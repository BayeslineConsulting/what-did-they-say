How it works

How we read the talks

A simple explanation of qualitative coding, the careful, line-by-line reading researchers use to find patterns, and how those patterns connect to what we were asked to do.

Read every lineAll 37 talks were divided into 932 short passages, each holding a single idea, and every one was read.
Tag what's saidEach passage got up to three short labels, called codes, naming what the speaker was doing: teaching a doctrine, telling a story, making a promise, extending an invitation. 124 codes in all.
Group the tagsCodes that belong together were grouped into categories, and categories into ten broader themes. The themes came from the talks, not from a list decided in advance.
Count with careEvery theme is backed by passages you can read. We count how many talks carried it, and in which sessions, so patterns rest on evidence rather than impressions.
Find every direct invitationSeparately, every sentence where a speaker directly invited, urged, asked or pleaded with us to do something was collected, checked word for word, and linked to the theme it fits.
Turn it into a planThose invitations become your six-month plan: choose a few, make them specific, and check in each week until the next conference.

The research methods behind it

None of this was invented for this site. It combines several established research methods, each with a name and published sources. Here they are in plain terms, and where each shows up.

A mixed-methods content analysis using Saldaña's two-cycle coding and codebook thematic analysis, with summative counts of scriptures, citations, and invitations tracked longitudinally across conferences.The approach, in one sentence, in the terms a researcher would use

Method (source)In plain termsWhere it shows up here
Qualitative content analysis
Mayring; Hsieh & Shannon		The umbrella name for the whole project: reading texts carefully and sorting what they say. Labels came from the talks themselves, and the counting came first, with interpretation after.The whole site
Two-cycle coding

Codebook thematic analysis

What this can show you

  • Which ideas came up again and again, across many speakers.
  • How the weekend moved, such as trials on Saturday and treating each other well on Sunday.
  • Every place a speaker directly asked us to act, in their exact words.
  • Related passages from different talks, side by side.

What it can't

  • Tell you what the Lord wants you to focus on. That comes through prayer and the Spirit.
  • Speak for the speakers or for the Church. This is an independent study.
  • Replace the talks. Read, watch, and listen to them in full.
  • Give the only right grouping. Another careful reader might organize things differently; the themes are lenses for pondering, not the final word.

What counts as a direct invitation

Only direct invitations count: sentences where a speaker says “I invite,” “let us,” “please,” “I urge,” “I plead,” “I ask you,” “I call upon,” or names what follows as an invitation. Plain instructions (“Keep your covenants”), statements of truth, stories, “may we” wishes, and descriptions are not counted, however inspiring; counting them would make the list too long to act on. This conference yielded 28 direct invitations from 22 talks, drawn from 50 exact quotations, beginning with President Oaks's 4. Each is shown in the speaker's exact words. Invitations that are alike are grouped, and every speaker is named. President Oaks's direct instructions that are not phrased as invitations are listed separately at the end of the plan page.
